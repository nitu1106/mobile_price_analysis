WHAT THE NUMBERS SAY
- Least precise statistic: 'share of 1 in blue' (interval width 8.8% of its value); most precise: 'correlation ram vs price_range'.
- Where a textbook formula exists, the bootstrap interval is broadly similar to it, with exceptions listed below (average end-point difference 19.2% of the interval width). The bootstrap needs no formula, so it also works for medians, percentiles and other statistics that have none.
- For 'std of battery_power' the formula interval differs clearly from the bootstrap (61% of the interval width): the formula assumes a bell-shaped column (or other conditions), while the bootstrap makes no shape assumption, so it is the safer interval to report here.

- Bootstrap distributions that are visibly lopsided (skewness above 0.3): none; the percentile, basic and BCa intervals are therefore almost the same.

STABILITY
- Number of resamples: the standard error settled (within 2% of its final value) after 2000 resamples at most; 5000 resamples are more than enough for these statistics (about 1000-2000 is a common default; use more for the tails of the distribution).
- Repeating the whole bootstrap 20 times: with 200 resamples the interval ends move by about 4.5% of the interval width, with 5000 resamples only 0.9%. This is pure Monte-Carlo noise; set a random seed to make results reproducible.
- Sample size: the interval width shrinks like 1/sqrt(n) (width x sqrt(n) stays near 4230); four times as much data halves the uncertainty.

WHEN THE BOOTSTRAP IS (NOT) ENOUGH
- It needs a representative, independent sample: it cannot fix a biased dataset, duplicated rows or time/group dependence (then resample whole groups).
- It estimates uncertainty of the SAMPLE statistic; it does not reveal values outside what the sample contains (e.g. the extreme tail of a distribution).
- For very small samples or extreme statistics (maximum, minimum) the bootstrap can fail.
- Percentile = simplest; basic = reflects it; BCa = best general choice (corrects bias and skew) but slower.
